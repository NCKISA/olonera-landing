The 40+ Checklist:
what to track, and
what to actually ask.

Most appointments in this decade end with "your labs are normal." This checklist exists so you walk in with specifics instead of a vague sense that something is off — and walk out with an actual plan.

Part 01

Part 02

Know your numbers before you go

You may already have many of these from past bloodwork. Gather what you have — and note what's missing, so you can ask whether it's worth checking.

  • Blood pressure Cardiovascular risk shifts in this decade; a recent reading is a useful baseline.
  • Full lipid panel Total, LDL, HDL, triglycerides — and the date it was taken.
  • HbA1c or fasting glucose Metabolic changes often show up here before anywhere else.
  • Thyroid panel (TSH, and free T4 if available) Thyroid issues mimic perimenopause closely enough to be worth ruling out.
  • Ferritin and complete blood count Relevant if your periods have become heavier or more frequent.
  • Vitamin D Commonly low, and relevant to bone health as estrogen declines.
  • Last bone-density scan, if you've had one Ask whether your history or risk factors make one worth discussing.
  • Current medications and supplements Full list, with doses — including anything over the counter.
A note on hormone testing: in perimenopause, hormone levels fluctuate enough day to day that a single blood test often can't confirm or rule out the transition. Diagnosis is usually made from your symptoms and cycle history. If a test is proposed, it's fair to ask what decision the result will change.
Part 03

Questions that get you a real answer

Pick the four or five that matter most to you and write them at the top of your notes. Appointments are short — the questions you ask first are the ones that get answered.

  • "Based on my symptoms and cycle history, do you think I'm in perimenopause?" Gets you a position instead of a deferral.
  • "What else could be causing this?" Thyroid, anemia, sleep apnea and depression overlap heavily. Ask what's been ruled out.
  • "What are all my options for this symptom — hormonal and non-hormonal?" Asks for the full menu rather than the first thing that comes to mind.
  • "Given my personal and family history, am I a candidate for hormone therapy?" If the answer is no, ask specifically which part of your history rules it out.
  • "What are the risks and benefits for someone with my history, specifically?" General statistics are a starting point, not an answer about you.
  • "If we try this, when should I expect to feel a difference — and what if I don't?" Sets a timeline and a follow-up, so a non-working plan doesn't quietly become permanent.
  • "Do any of my supplements interact with what you're suggesting?" This is exactly why you brought the full list.
  • "What should I be doing now to protect my bones and heart?" Moves the visit from symptom relief to the next thirty years.
  • "If this isn't your area, who do you recommend I see?" A fair question, and often the most productive one in the room.
Part 04

Don't wait for an appointment if

Some symptoms overlap with perimenopause but need to be looked at sooner rather than at your next routine visit.

Contact a clinician promptly about any of these

  • Bleeding after you have gone 12 months with no period
  • Periods so heavy you soak through protection hourly, or that last beyond seven days
  • Bleeding between periods, or after sex
  • Chest pain, severe shortness of breath, or fainting
  • A new severe headache, or one unlike any you've had before
  • Calf pain or swelling on one side
  • Thoughts of harming yourself, or a mood change that frightens you
This list is not exhaustive. Anything that feels sharply wrong to you is worth a call, whether or not it appears here.
